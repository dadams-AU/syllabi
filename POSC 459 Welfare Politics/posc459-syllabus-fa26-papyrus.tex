% ========== SECTION 5: COURSE DESCRIPTION ==========
\section*{Course Description}

{\setlength{\parindent}{0pt}\setlength{\parskip}{0.6\baselineskip}%
This course is an intensive introduction to U.S. social welfare policy and politics. It has four parts: the big picture (who are the poor, and how did the welfare state develop?); the programs (social insurance, the safety net, tax expenditures); two case studies in major reform (welfare reform and the ACA); and the political forces that shape all of it (public opinion, interest groups, race, and gender).

The course moves between levels of analysis. Comparative welfare state theory sits alongside individual family narratives; legislative history sits alongside street-level program design. Both matter. You cannot understand why TANF looks the way it does without understanding the politics that produced it, and you cannot evaluate whether the ACA succeeded without understanding what it was actually trying to accomplish.

This course is taught from a political science and policy analysis perspective. Political science helps explain \emph{why} we get the policies we get; policy analysis helps evaluate \emph{whether those policies do what they claim}. Both lenses are at work throughout the semester.
}

% ========== SECTION 6: STUDENT LEARNING OUTCOMES ==========
\section*{Student Learning Outcomes}

{\setlength{\parindent}{0pt}\setlength{\parskip}{0.6\baselineskip}%
By the end of the semester, students will be able to:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Describe the core architecture of the U.S. welfare state: social insurance, public assistance, tax expenditures, and public-private arrangements.
    \item Trace the political development of major programs (Social Security, TANF, Medicaid, ACA) and connect that history to current policy debates.
    \item Apply key concepts from political science and policy analysis to evaluate welfare programs: policy feedback, institutional design, interest group politics, and public opinion.
    \item Analyze how race and gender have structured both program design and access to benefits.
    \item Situate U.S. welfare policy within a comparative frame and explain the sources of American exceptionalism.
    \item Construct an evidence-based policy argument using primary sources and scholarly literature.
\end{enumerate}
}

% ========== SECTION 9: COURSE REQUIREMENTS ==========
\section*{Course Requirements}

\subsection*{Course Format}
This is an in-person lecture and discussion course meeting twice weekly for 75 minutes. Students complete assigned readings before class; class time is divided between lecture, structured discussion, and periodic documentary screenings. Three weeks are structured as asynchronous work weeks with no in-person meetings (see below).

\subsection*{Three Asynchronous Weeks}
Three weeks are designated asynchronous. There are no in-person meetings those weeks. In Weeks 4 and 9, students complete the assigned readings, watch a designated documentary, and submit a Canvas response by that Friday at 11:59 p.m. Week 14 carries no new deliverable: it is reading and independent work time, with term papers and graduate outlines due that Wednesday. Specific instructions will be posted to Canvas in advance.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Week 4 (Async):} Political development of the welfare state; \emph{Growing Up Poor in America}, Part 2
    \item \textbf{Week 9 (Async):} Welfare reform; \emph{Two American Families}
    \item \textbf{Week 14 (Async):} Public opinion and interest groups; term papers and grad outlines due. No documentary and no Canvas response---the week is reading and independent work
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Graded Work}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Required for all students (60\% total):} Attendance and Participation (10\%); Discussion Papers, 5 of 10 (10\%); Midterm Exam (20\%); Final Exam (20\%).
    \item \textbf{Undergraduates only (40\% total):}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Policy Brief (20\% across three stages):} Program selection (credit/no credit, due Monday September 21); a source and claims memo (5\%, due Friday October 9); and the brief itself (15\%, due Wednesday October 28). A 3--4 page analysis of one program: OASDI, SSI, TANF, Unemployment Insurance, Medicaid, Medicare, SNAP, or the EITC. Cover the program's stated purpose, who it serves, its major design features, and one significant reform debate. Academic writing conventions and source citations required.
        \item \textbf{Term Paper (20\%):} A 6--8 page research paper building on the policy brief. Analyze the political and policy-relevant strengths and weaknesses of your program and develop a reform proposal, drawing on course concepts and the program's political history.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Graduate students only (40\% total):}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Research Proposal (10\%):} Topic, research question, and preliminary bibliography.
        \item \textbf{Introduction/Outline/Annotated Bibliography (10\%):} Substantial revision from the proposal with clear research design and sources.
        \item \textbf{Final Research Paper (20\%):} A 10--15 page original research paper due Monday, December 14, at the start of the final exam.
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Graduate Student Requirements}
Graduate students complete all readings including graduate-only readings posted on Canvas. They take both the midterm and final exams and write five discussion papers. In place of the undergraduate policy brief and term paper, graduate students complete a scaffolded 10--15 page original research paper sequence with staged feedback and revision across three milestones:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Research Proposal (10\%)} (1--2 pages): Due Week 7, October 5.
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Identify policy topic, core research question, and why the question matters for welfare politics/policy.
        \item Include a working thesis or argument direction.
        \item Include a preliminary bibliography (minimum 6 credible sources, including scholarly sources).
        \item Instructor feedback will focus on scope, feasibility, and argument focus.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Introduction, Outline, and Annotated Bibliography (10\%):} Due Week 14, December 2.
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Revised introduction with clear thesis and paper roadmap.
        \item Detailed section outline showing claim flow and supporting evidence for each section.
        \item Annotated bibliography with brief annotations describing each source's argument and relevance to your project.
        \item Substantial revision from the proposal is expected; this milestone should show a sharpened question and stronger evidence base.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Final Research Paper (20\%)} (10--15 pages): Due Monday, December 14, at the start of the final exam.
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Original analysis that synthesizes course concepts, scholarly literature, and policy evidence.
        \item Clear argument structure (thesis, evidence, analysis, counterargument/limitations, conclusion).
        \item Proper citation and academic writing conventions.
        \item The final submission should directly incorporate and respond to feedback from Milestones 1 and 2.
    \end{itemize}
\end{enumerate}

% ========== SECTION 12: POLICY ON GENERATIVE AI ==========
\section*{Policy on the Use of Generative AI and Other Technology}

Generative AI is permitted in this course, but use must be transparent, intentional, and in service of learning. The core principle is simple: \textbf{you must do the intellectual work of this course}. AI can amplify your thinking, but not replace it.

\vspace{0.5em}
\noindent\textbf{Short version:} if a tool suggests new words and you use them, that counts as AI use under this policy.

\vspace{0.5em}
\noindent \textbf{The test.} Most tools now flag problems \emph{and} offer to fix them, so what matters is what the tool hands you. If it only flags a possible problem, that is basic mechanics---spellcheck, punctuation, dictionary and thesaurus lookup, grammar flags---which is always fine and never needs disclosure. If it proposes new wording, a summary, or a rewritten version for you to adopt, this policy covers it. \textbf{AI may identify problems in your writing. It may not supply the prose that fixes them.}

\vspace{0.5em}
\noindent \textbf{This includes AI built into tools you already use}, whether or not you switched it on: Smart Compose and ``Help me write,'' Word Copilot, Apple Intelligence, AI search summaries, and the rewriting half of Grammarly. What counts is what you adopt. You do not have to feel like you ``used AI'' for it to count. If you are unsure whether something counts, \textbf{ask before you submit}---a good-faith question asked in advance is treated as exactly that, and it does not put you under suspicion.

\subsection*{Assignment labels}

Every assignment carries one of three labels, stated on the assignment itself. You should never have to infer it.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{GREEN} --- AI permitted within the limits below. No disclosure needed.
\item \textbf{YELLOW} --- Permitted within stated limits. \textbf{Disclosure required.}
\item \textbf{RED} --- No AI. The assignment is designed to be completed without it, and doing so is the point.
\end{itemize}

\noindent \textbf{Red assignments in this course:} the Week 2 baseline writing diagnostic, all in-class writing, the midterm exam, and the final exam. Everything else is Green or Yellow; the Canvas policy page lists every assignment by label. \textbf{If an assignment has no label}, treat it as Yellow and ask me. An unlabeled assignment is my oversight, not an opening.

\vspace{0.5em}
\noindent Red assignments are not punishments or trust tests. Some capacities only develop when you build them yourself, and I need to see your unaided thinking at least once to be useful to you for the rest of the term.

\subsection*{Permitted and not permitted}

\noindent \textbf{Permitted:} brainstorming, and working out an outline before you have written the piece; explaining concepts you don't understand, then explaining them back in your own words; locating sources, which you then read yourself; diagnosing problems in your clarity, grammar, or organization; sanity-checking your analysis or logic; generating synthetic examples or test cases for your ideas.

\vspace{0.5em}
\noindent \textbf{Not permitted:} using AI to generate your analysis, arguments, or conclusions; submitting AI-generated text as your own writing; using AI to avoid engaging with course concepts or readings; letting AI do the intellectual heavy lifting---interpreting sources, building arguments, synthesizing ideas. Uploading an assigned reading for a tool to summarize or explain is not permitted unless I explicitly allow it.

\subsection*{Disclosure}

On Yellow work, add one or two sentences at the end of the assignment: what tool, and what you used it for. \textbf{You do not need to attach prompts, transcripts, or logs unless an assignment specifically asks for them}, and if one does, it will say so. Disclosure is a description of your process, the same way a methods section describes how a study was run. It is not a confession. Disclosing use has never lowered a grade in my courses and it is not going to start. What creates a problem is undisclosed use that shows up in the work. And it does show up.

\subsection*{When the policy is broken}

\noindent \textbf{Consequences}, proportionate to what happened: a conversation and a revision requirement; a grade penalty; or, for serious or repeated violations, a formal referral under CSUF's Academic Dishonesty Policy (UPS 300.021), which can carry sanctions up to dismissal. Forgetting a disclosure sentence on otherwise honest work is an omission. Passing off generated analysis as your own is deception. They land in very different places.

\vspace{0.5em}
\noindent \textbf{On detection.} I am not going to pretend I can catch everything with software, and \textbf{I will not run your writing through an AI detector and treat the output as evidence}. Those tools are unreliable, and their errors are not evenly distributed. They flag multilingual writers and plain prose at higher rates. What I will do is read carefully, ask you to explain your process, and make judgments based on the work, the assignment rules, and that conversation. \emph{Walk me through how you got here.} If you did the thinking, that is an easy conversation and often a good one.

\subsection*{The full policy is on Canvas}
